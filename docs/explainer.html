Sufficit  ·  from the Latin sufficere, “it suffices”

A computer that knows how much it doesn’t need to know

An illustrated tour of a small research code that answers physics questions with proofs attached — and is honest about what it cannot do.

Ask a supercomputer for tomorrow’s temperature and it will happily churn through billions of numbers overnight to hand you one: 23°. Nearly everything it computed was detail nobody asked about. This is not a flaw in one weather model — it is the standard shape of scientific computing. We simulate everything, then ask a question whose answer would fit on a postage stamp.

Part 1Most of the work is wasted — famously

The interesting thing is what happens when someone notices. Every decade or so, a scientist realizes that in their particular field, the map from “full simulation” to “the number I wanted” throws almost everything away — and finds coordinates where the throwing-away is done first. The fast multipole method turned gravity calculations from quadratic to linear cost by noticing that a distant cluster of stars pulls on you almost exactly like one fat star. Each such discovery costs years of a brilliant career, and the trick usually stays locked inside one community’s software.

Sufficit treats that discovery process as a compilation problem. You declare three things — the physical model, the question, and the error you can tolerate — and the system searches for a cheap computation that answers the question and proves how wrong it could be. That last clause is the whole project.

Part 2An answer with a receipt

Every result in Sufficit is a little package: a value, an error bar, and the paper trail of how that error bar was earned. The crucial point is what kind of thing the error bar is. It is not a statistical hunch, not a “typically accurate to…” from the manual. It is a theorem: the true answer lies within this interval. When two certified numbers are added, their error bars add. When one passes through a well-behaved function, the bar stretches by a known factor. Errors propagate through programs the way units do — mechanically, with no judgment calls.

The most satisfying form this takes is the bracket: squeeze the truth from both sides at once.

For the hydrogen molecule, computed from scratch — the electron integrals, the quantum mechanics, everything — the bracket comes out to −1.137275944 ± 0.00000000000015 hartree. The width of that interval is ten billion times smaller than the precision chemists call “chemical accuracy.” And it is a promise, not an estimate.

Part 3Guess freely, check strictly

Here is the engineering trick that makes the whole thing practical, and it is worth stating as a slogan: it does not matter how you find an answer; it matters how you check it.

Three examples of checks that cannot be argued with. To prove an energy ceiling, plug any trial state into the energy formula — the variational theorem guarantees the true ground energy is below it. To prove a floor, ask a yes/no question the matrix cannot lie about: “is H − c·I positive?” — a Cholesky factorization either completes or it doesn’t, and each answer moves a bisection one step. And when a check itself needs to gamble (random probing of a matrix too big to look at entry by entry), the odds of being fooled are computed and printed on the certificate: fail_p = 10⁻¹⁰. Not hidden. Printed.

This separation is why the code is full of cheerfully disreputable machinery — subgradient bundles, randomized sketches, greedy searches — none of which is ever trusted. The moment an optimizer finishes, its work is re-derived through a check that would have caught it lying.

Part 4Big things from small windows

The deepest trick is how a laptop puts rigorous bounds on systems that are, by any direct accounting, impossibly large. A chain of 2000 hydrogen atoms has 42000 quantum states — a number with more digits than there are atoms in the universe. Sufficit brackets its energy anyway, in seconds. How?

The energy of the whole is at least the sum of the windows’ certified floors — that inequality is exact, and each window is small enough to handle honestly. The cost depends on the window size, not the chain length. A 20-atom chain and a 2000-atom chain cost the same. What you lose is tightness: the windows cannot see correlations longer than themselves, and the certificate is wide by exactly the amount of physics that escapes. Which brings us to the knob.

Part 5The knob: pay for precision

Everything in Sufficit is built around one economic principle: cost should scale with the precision of the question, not the sharpness of the formalism. Want a rough answer? Cheap. Want a tight one? One honest footnote a chart won’t tell you: how fast the knob pays off depends on the physics. Systems with an energy gap forget their surroundings exponentially fast, so the bracket collapses quickly. Critical systems — poised at a phase transition, correlations reaching everywhere — give up their precision grudgingly, a factor of two per turn. The chains above are the hard, grudging kind. The certificate doesn’t hide this; it is this.

Part 6On not fooling yourself

“The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool.”
— R. P. Feynman

A peculiarity of this codebase: its failures are kept, deliberately, as permanent tests. Three exhibits from the museum:

The demodulation that did nothing. A clever-looking trick for high-frequency wave scattering — factor out the oscillation, compress what remains — was implemented, measured, and found to change the results by exactly zero. The reason is a two-line theorem: the trick is a rotation, and rotations don’t change the quantities being compressed. The theorem now lives in a test, so the idea can never be expensively re-discovered.

The textbook update that made things worse. A standard “optimal” rebalancing rule for error budgets degraded the bound when measured — it optimizes against yesterday’s answer, and the system moves. The shipped version is provably unable to end worse than it began; the measured optimum turned out to be the starting point. That negative result is also a test.

The warm start that poisoned the well. Reusing the previous step’s answer to speed up an iterative solver — the most natural optimization in the world — quietly biased its output and loosened the certificates by a third. Measured, diagnosed, removed, and pinned in a comment at the scene of the crime.

None of these could have corrupted a certificate — the checking lane doesn’t care where guesses come from. But each would have wasted tightness or compute forever if it hadn’t been caught by the habit of measuring everything.

Part 8Where this goes

The near-term map is concrete. A machine with 96 GB of memory unlocks the next window size — and with it the community’s standard hard benchmark for strongly-correlated matter, with two-sided certificates that don’t currently exist in the literature. A consumer GPU unlocks the size after that, through a pleasingly honest trick: run the proofs in fast low precision and carry the rounding error in the certificate, where it turns out to be ten thousand times smaller than anything it could obscure. Beyond that, the bottleneck is mathematics again, which is as it should be.

The long game is the one the founding document states: every compression discovered becomes a reusable, certified rewrite rule, so that an insight paid for once — in any field — compounds everywhere, the way compiler optimizations do. The fast multipole method was the existence proof that physics answers are compressible. This project is the induction step.

Sufficit is a working research code: one Python file of certified rewrites, one file of tests in which every claimed bound is checked against brute force, exact solutions, or independent constructions — 79 checks and counting, several of which exist to commemorate a mistake.
