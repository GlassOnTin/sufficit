Sufficit  ·  from the Latin sufficere, “it suffices”

A computer that knows how much it doesn’t need to know

A tour of a small research code that answers physics questions with proofs attached, and says plainly what it cannot do.

Ask a supercomputer for tomorrow’s temperature and it computes billions of numbers overnight to give you one: 23°. Almost everything it computed was detail nobody asked about. That is the standard shape of scientific computing: simulate everything, then ask a question whose answer fits on a postage stamp.

Part 1Most of the work is wasted

Every decade or so, someone notices that in their field the map from full simulation to final answer throws almost everything away, and finds coordinates where the throwing-away happens first. The fast multipole method made gravity sums linear instead of quadratic: a distant cluster of stars pulls on you almost exactly like one heavy point. A discovery like that costs years, and it usually stays locked inside one community’s software.

Sufficit treats the discovery as a compilation problem. You declare three things: the physical model, the question, and the error you can tolerate. The system searches for a cheap computation that answers the question and proves how wrong it could be. The proof is the product.

Part 2An answer with a receipt

Every result is a value, an error bound, and a record of how the bound was earned. The bound is not a hunch. It is a statement of fact: the true answer lies inside this interval. Certified numbers compose. Add two of them and the bounds add. Pass one through a well-behaved function and the bound stretches by a known factor. Errors move through a program the way units do, mechanically.

The best form this takes is the bracket: squeeze the truth from both sides at once.

For the hydrogen molecule in the smallest standard basis, computed from scratch, the bracket is −1.137275944 ± 0.00000000000015 hartree. The interval is ten billion times narrower than what chemists call chemical accuracy, and it is a guarantee, not an estimate. Be clear about what is guaranteed: the answer of the declared model. The basis itself sits about 0.05 hartree from the real molecule. Every certificate on this site bounds the model's answer, not the world's; the model is always stated, and choosing a better one is a separate, honest step.

Part 3Guess freely, check strictly

One rule makes the whole thing practical: it does not matter how you find an answer; it matters how you check it. Optimizers, randomized sketches, and greedy searches produce the guesses, and none of them is trusted. The certificate comes from a check that does not care where the guess came from.

Three checks that cannot be argued with. Plug any trial state into the energy formula and the true ground energy is below the result. Ask whether H − c·I has a Cholesky factorization; it either completes or it does not, and each answer moves a bisection one step. And when a check must gamble, because the matrix is too large to inspect entry by entry, the odds of being fooled are computed and printed on the certificate: fail_p = 10⁻¹⁰.

Part 4Big things from small windows

A chain of 2000 hydrogen atoms has 42000 quantum states, a number with more digits than there are atoms in the universe. Sufficit brackets its energy in a few seconds. The reason is locality. Slide a small window along the chain and weight each interaction by the number of windows that contain it. The windows then add up to exactly the whole system, and the true energy is at least the sum of the certified window minima.

The cost depends on the window size, not the chain length. A 20-atom chain and a 2000-atom chain cost the same. What you lose is tightness: a window cannot see correlations longer than itself, and the certificate widens by exactly the physics that escapes.

Part 5Pay for precision

Cost should scale with the precision of the question. The knob here is the window length ℓ. Each unit of ℓ costs a factor of four in computer time and buys roughly a factor of two in certified width. The chart shows measurements, not projections.

How fast the knob pays depends on the physics. Systems with an energy gap forget their surroundings exponentially fast, and the bracket collapses quickly. Critical systems give up a factor of two per turn. These chains are the critical kind, and the certificate reports exactly that.

Part 6Three kinds of honesty

Not every honest answer is a theorem. Each certificate carries a tier that says what kind of statement it is, and when certified numbers combine, the result keeps the weakest tier in the chain. Arithmetic never upgrades a guarantee.

Part 7Refusal is an answer

Some questions have no affordable certificate, and the honest output is a refusal that says why. The refusals are specific. The cluster expansion refuses beyond the radius where its convergence is proven. The light-cone simulation refuses when the excitation front outruns the largest affordable cone, and its error message prices the next one.

Part 8On not fooling yourself

“The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool.”
— R. P. Feynman

The failures are kept, deliberately, as permanent tests. A clever demodulation for wave scattering changed the results by exactly zero; the two-line theorem that explains why is now a test, so the idea cannot be expensively rediscovered. A textbook rebalancing rule made the bound worse when measured; the shipped version provably cannot end worse than it started. A warm start biased an iterative solver and loosened the certificates by a third; it was measured, removed, and pinned in a comment. None of these could have corrupted a certificate. Each would have wasted computer time forever if it had not been measured.

Part 9Nine domains, one method

The target list names nine fields where tolerances are already part of the culture: chemists state chemical accuracy, gravitational-wave detectors state a mismatch, lattice theorists publish error budgets. All nine now have entry rewrites, and each has a case page that regenerates from a fresh run on every push: the idea, the program rendered from the module, and the result of running it.

Part 10What you have to trust

A certificate is only as good as its checker, so here is the trusted base, stated. When a page says RIGOROUS, you are trusting four things: the theorems quoted in the docstrings, which are hand-proved, not machine-checked; a few hundred lines of margin bookkeeping that execute those theorems in floating point, padding every rounding step in the safe direction; NumPy and LAPACK, which do the arithmetic the margins surround; and Python's exact rational arithmetic, for the checks that run over the rationals and never touch a float. A bug in the bookkeeping would corrupt every certificate downstream of it. That is the real attack surface, and the test suite is the current defense: every bound is compared against brute force, exact solutions, or independent constructions. The honest gap: none of it is machine-checked. A proof-assistant kernel for the handful of core inequalities would shrink the trusted base from a module to a page, and it is the obvious next hardening.

Sufficit is a working research code: one Python module of certified rewrites and one test suite in which every claimed bound is checked against brute force, exact solutions, or independent constructions. 119 checks, several of which exist to record a mistake. Source on GitHub, AGPL-3.0.
